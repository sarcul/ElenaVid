# Elena

### *Tragedia lirica in tre atti*

## Treatment (Working Document)

Title confirmed: *Elena*. Formal genre designation: *tragedia lirica in tre atti* (lyric tragedy in three acts), following the tradition of Bellini's *Norma*.

The character names *Marco*, *Vittorio*, *Davide*, and *Tommaso* remain placeholders and may still be revised.

## Premise

A woman, trapped in a brutal marriage and mother to a disabled child, escapes to find unexpected happiness with an unassuming, decent man. The reappearance of the boy she once loved as a youth reactivates a longing she had buried, and the affair that follows coincides with her child's death. She loses all three relationships and is left alone with the full weight of one decision, made at twenty to a man who seemed the love of her life, from which everything that followed was already determined.

## Period and Setting

Italy, 1948 to 1958. The decade matters: post-war reconstruction, Catholic moral framework still dominant, divorce illegal until 1970, women's social mobility severely constrained, no infrastructure for disabled children. A woman who leaves her husband in 1953 with a disabled child is doing something genuinely transgressive and dangerous.

Three locations, each with distinct musical-visual atmosphere:

- **Act I**: A village in the Veneto countryside (rural, traditional, parental authority); then a grand industrialist's house in Verona (cold, formal, gilded)
- **Act II**: Trieste (port city, liminal, Mitteleuropean, slightly outside Italy proper, a place of escape and second chances)
- **Act III**: Trieste

## Characters and Voice Types

| Character | Voice | Description |
|-----------|-------|-------------|
| Elena | Lyric soprano | The heroine. 18 in Act I, 28 by close. Lyrical line throughout, fracturing in Act III. |
| Marco | Tenor | The friend of her youth. Decent, intelligent, becomes a doctor. Catalyst not seducer, but morally implicated: he knows about Tommaso and Davide before he and Elena become lovers. |
| Vittorio | Baritone | The cruel husband. Wealthy, controlling, capable of violence. Should not be cartoonish. Physically present in Act I; returns on stage as singing visions in Act II Scene 2 and Act III Scene 1; returns as an offstage voice in Act III Scene 3. Substantial role across all three acts. |
| Davide | Bass-baritone | The man she finds in Trieste. A schoolteacher. Understated warmth, real depth. In Act III his grief at Tommaso's death and at the sensed loss of Elena breaks into one moment of ungoverned heat, not cruelty but raw human grief, which Elena, her perception calibrated by Vittorio, misreads as threat. He must never actually be frightening to anyone but her. |
| Tommaso | Single sustained tone (boy treble or instrumental surrogate) | The disabled child, present but profoundly limited. Carried or wheeled on stage in many scenes. His "voice" is a single sustained tone, returning at moments of his presence throughout Acts II and III. The tone may be sung by the child himself (a true treble) or carried in the orchestra (a held flute, oboe, or muted trumpet) when the child is present but not vocalising. In Act III Scene 2, the tone fails to return where the audience expects it. That silence is his death. |
| Elena's mother | Mezzo-soprano | Act I only. Loving but pragmatic, urges the marriage. |
| A neighbour / confidante in Trieste | Mezzo-soprano | Witness figure across Acts II and III. |
| Chorus | SATB | Triple function: villagers (Act I), unseen moral witnesses (Act III Scene 1), congregation at the funeral (Act III Scene 2 or 3). The same body of voices wears different masks across the work. |

The vocal contrast between Marco (tenor, idealised, of the past) and Davide (bass-baritone, grounded, of the present) is structurally important. The audience should feel Davide is not a consolation prize.

## Synopsis

### Act I — Youth and Entrapment

**Scene 1.** *Village square, Veneto. Summer 1948.*

The scene opens with the chorus as villagers, going about a market day. This first appearance establishes them as a presence in Elena's world: the village, the eyes, the inheritance of expectation. Elena (18) and Marco (20), friends since childhood, emerge from the crowd. A duet of farewell: Marco is leaving for Bologna to study medicine. The duet's surface is light; underneath, on Elena's side, is something she cannot name. They part with a vague promise to write. The villagers thin out. Elena alone: her first aria, a moment of suspended longing for something she does not yet understand she has lost.

**Scene 2.** *Elena's family parlour, then a church. Spring 1950.*

Two years on. No word from Marco. A match has been proposed: Vittorio, son of a Verona industrialist. He is prosperous, well-regarded, courteous, from a good family, and on every visible measure an excellent man. Elena's mother, who loves her, sings in favour of the match, of the security and the future it offers. This must not play as a mother pressing a reluctant daughter into a grey bargain. Elena is not being made to settle. The dramatic point, essential to the whole opera, is that Vittorio genuinely appears to be everything a young woman of her time and place could hope for. Elena's aria here is not resignation. It is cautious, real hope. She has stopped waiting for the unnamed thing Marco represented, but she does not marry Vittorio as a consolation; she marries him because the man before her seems, on all the evidence she has, the right and even the fortunate choice. There is no warning for her to read, and the audience must see that there is none. Nobody in the scene, including the audience, can find the flaw, because at this point in time it is not visible.

The scene transitions to the wedding itself. The chorus returns, now as wedding guests and the social weight of the village, singing a celebratory chorus. **This is the central tune of the opera.** It must be at the level of *Nessun Dorma* or *La donna è mobile*: a melody the audience leaves the house humming, a melody that becomes culturally portable. Compose it before the libretto is finished. Everything else in the score depends on this theme being strong, because it returns transformed and lamenting in Act III Scene 1 during the consummation, and the entire dramatic structure of that later scene depends on the audience recognising the melody under the Latin lament. If the wedding tune is forgettable, the consummation scene loses half its power.

The scene closes as the chorus's celebration modulates, almost imperceptibly, into something colder, and Elena turns to face Vittorio for the first time as his wife.

**Scene 3.** *The Verona house. Autumn 1953.*

Three years of marriage. Tommaso, two years old, is severely disabled. Vittorio resents the child openly and Elena increasingly. The scene's centre is Elena's aria to her sleeping son: love absolute and unconditional, set against her own ruined life. Vittorio enters drunk, cruel; an altercation; he strikes her. The servant looks away. Elena resolves to leave. Curtain.

### Act II — Escape and Tenderness

**Scene 1.** *A railway platform at night. Days later.*

Elena with Tommaso in her arms, a single suitcase. She is travelling to Trieste, where a distant cousin has offered a room. Her aria here is the structural opposite of Scene 1's farewell: she is leaving rather than being left, and she carries with her the thing she most loves. Train sounds in the orchestra.

**Scene 2.** *A modest apartment building, Trieste. 1955.*

Eighteen months later. Elena works as a seamstress. Davide, a teacher who lives in the same building, has slowly befriended her. He plays with Tommaso, helps her on the stairs, asks nothing.

The scene's first event, however, is a nightmare. Elena alone in her room at night. Vittorio appears to her in vision, embodied on stage as a singing baritone (not projected, not recorded). He sings a brief phrase, set to the melody that will return identically in Act III Scene 1 and Act III Scene 3 offstage. At this first appearance the words are those of a husband's claim, cold but almost tender in their cadence. Draft text (indicative, to be refined):

> *Elena, sei mia per sempre.*
> *Nel tuo cuore io rimango.*
> *Nessun luogo ti nasconde*
> *dal tuo sposo, tuo signore.*

(Elena, you are mine forever. / In your heart I remain. / No place hides you / from your husband, your lord.)

Four ottonari, stresses falling where the music requires. She wakes in fear. This brief sequence (perhaps three to four minutes) plants the device of Vittorio's returns and reminds the audience that her freedom is provisional.

The body of the scene then unfolds: a domestic trio (Elena, Davide, Tommaso with one or two wordless vocalisations). Davide's aria of love is unshowy, almost shy: he knows what he is offering and knows it is not romance in the operatic sense, and he offers it anyway. Elena accepts. A love duet, restrained, that closes the scene.

**Scene 3.** *A street in Trieste. 1957.*

Two years on. Elena and Davide live together as husband and wife in everything but law (she is still legally married to Vittorio). Tommaso is more fragile but alive and adored. Elena, on an errand, encounters Marco. He has just taken a hospital position in the city. Their reunion is unrehearsed and electric.

Crucially, in this scene Elena tells Marco the truth: that she has a disabled son, that she lives with another man, that her old life is gone. She tells him because she must, because to lie now would make any future contact a deception from the start. Marco receives this. He does not flee. They agree to meet again, ostensibly as friends. The duet that closes the act is therefore not innocent. Both of them know what they are choosing to begin. Curtain.

### Act III — Return and Ruin

**Scene 1.** *Various locations across several months. Then Marco's apartment, night.*

The scene's first half is built from short episodes (could be staged with minimal scenery and lighting changes): Elena and Marco walking, talking, having coffee. Each meeting longer than the last. Interleaved with scenes of Elena returning home to Davide, distracted, lying by omission. The orchestral writing should make the moral drift audible: Marco's motif from Act I, transformed, increasingly dominant. Marco himself has an aria of unease: he sees what is happening, knows he should withdraw, cannot. He knows about Tommaso. He knows about Davide. He stays anyway.

A turning point: Elena alone, perhaps in church or on a Trieste street at dusk. Vittorio appears to her in vision again, embodied on stage as in Act II. He sings to the same melody. The music is identical. The words have descended. Draft text:

> *Elena, mi porti dentro.*
> *Come ferro nel tuo petto.*
> *Ovunque vai, io ti abito,*
> *tuo padrone, tuo respiro.*

(Elena, you carry me inside you. / Like iron in your breast. / Wherever you go, I inhabit you, / your master, your breath.)

Same metre, same stress pattern, same music. Only the meaning has shifted: from husband to parasite, from "I remain in your heart" to "I inhabit you." The vision dissolves. Elena, in the wake of it, turns toward Marco. She does not go to him out of desire alone, nor out of decision in any meaningful sense. She goes because the trap has closed around her and Marco is the one available doorway. The vision has confirmed what she has known since Act I Scene 3: that the marriage made her Vittorio's and that nothing will undo it. If she is already inhabited, she takes what she can of what she wanted before she was.

The scene then moves to Marco's apartment at night. The lovers approach one another, the lights narrow, and the chorus enters, unseen. The audience does not see the consummation itself; it sees the lovers in shadow or silhouette, or in a stylised tableau, or simply the empty room with their voices fading. What dominates the stage is the chorus.

The chorus here laments. It does not condemn Elena. It mourns her, and mourns with her, because the audience and the chorus together know what she does not yet know: that this moment is the hinge on which everything she loves will turn. The text is the **Stabat Mater** in Latin, five verses selected from the twenty, set against the wedding chorus from Act I returning slowed and harmonically darkened beneath. Two laments overlay: the lament for the marriage Elena once made, and the lament for the child she is about to lose.

The five verses, in performance order:

**Movement 1.** *Stabat mater dolorosa / iuxta crucem lacrimosa, / dum pendebat Filius.* (Verse 1.) The sorrowful mother stood weeping beside the cross while her Son hung there. Opens the chorus. Establishes the lament. The wedding melody enters beneath, barely recognisable, in the lower strings.

**Movement 2.** *Cuius animam gementem, / contristatam et dolentem / pertransivit gladius.* (Verse 2.) Through her groaning, sorrowing soul a sword passed. The climactic moment, set beneath or around the orchestral consummation. The word *gladius* (sword) should land as a harmonic event, not a dynamic one: a shift in the harmony rather than a crescendo. The wedding melody fragments here.

**Movement 3.** *Vidit suum dulcem natum / moriendo desolatum, / dum emisit spiritum.* (Verse 8.) She saw her sweet Son dying, abandoned, as he gave up his spirit. The direct prefiguring of Tommaso's death. This verse is the one that will resonate most powerfully in retrospect, once the audience reaches Scene 2 and hears Tommaso's sustained tone fail to return. The word *desolatum* should be set with weight.

**Movement 4.** *Eia, Mater, fons amoris, / me sentire vim doloris / fac, ut tecum lugeam.* (Verse 9.) O Mother, fount of love, make me feel the force of your grief, that I may mourn with you. The pivot. The chorus shifts from narrating Mary's sorrow to asking to share in it. This is the chorus speaking for the audience, asking to grieve alongside Elena for what is about to happen. The wedding melody returns here, more fully stated, in a minor transformation.

**Movement 5.** *Iuxta crucem tecum stare, / et me tibi sociare / in planctu desidero.* (Verse 14.) To stand beside the cross with you, and to join myself to you in lamentation, this I desire. The vow. The chorus commits to witnessing Elena's suffering. This closes the choral sequence and should fade into orchestral silence, the wedding melody dissolving into a single held chord, unresolved.

The scene ends as the chorus fades and the orchestra holds a single chord, unresolved. Curtain or blackout.

**Scene 2.** *A restaurant or a street in Trieste, evening. Then the apartment.*

Elena and Davide are out together. An ordinary evening, the first time in the act the audience sees them simply at ease, which is what makes what follows worse. A short passage of unremarkable tenderness between them. Then a neighbour, or the woman who cares for Tommaso during their absences, reaches them with word that the child has collapsed. They go home at once.

They arrive in time. Tommaso is dying, a crisis of his congenital condition, unconnected to anyone's presence or absence. Both parents are with him. Elena holds her son as he dies.

This is the one moment in the opera that is not compromised, not divided, not corrupted by secrecy or guilt. Elena loves her child completely and her grief is whole. The ensemble over the dying child is shared between Elena and Davide, two people in undivided sorrow, and for the length of it they are not a woman with a secret and the man she deceives; they are simply a mother and the man who has been a father to her son. The orchestra carries Tommaso's sustained tone. The tone fails to return where the audience expects it. That silence is his death.

The purity of this grief is structural, not incidental. Elena is given, once, something clean. It is precisely that cleanness that makes Scene 3 unbearable. What destroys her is not punishment arriving on schedule. It is pure loss becoming, by its own weight, the thing that breaks every wall she has held.

**Scene 3.** *A church, then the apartment. Days later.*

The scene opens with the funeral. The chorus returns a final time, now as the Catholic congregation, singing fragments of the Requiem. This is the third and final use of the chorus, completing the structural arc: villagers (innocence and inheritance) → unseen lamenting witnesses to the consummation → mourners at the funeral. The Latin should be set simply. Tommaso's sustained tone weaves through the orchestra under the chorus, then disappears for the last time.

The action shifts to the apartment after the mourners have left. A letter arrives, from Marco. He wrote it without knowing the child had died: an outpouring of his feelings for Elena, posted into what he could not have known would be the worst possible moment. The letter is Marco's structural counterpart to Elena's wedding, a single act, not malicious, that detonates everything. Catastrophic timing, not cruelty. He is the catalyst to the last; he is never the author of the ruin.

Davide reaches the letter first. He reads it in silence, or reads a single line aloud and then stops. The letter is not performed: Marco's words are not floated over the scene by an offstage voice, because the moment belongs to Elena and Davide and not to Marco's feelings. What the audience watches is Davide's face. What they hear is the orchestra beneath the silence. Elena sees comprehension arrive in him before she knows what he is holding. The terrible recognition is wordless.

Then Elena's collapse. She does not deny. She does not explain the letter away. She does not manage the moment, because grief has taken from her the strength that the keeping of secrets requires. She tells Davide everything. The letter has not caused this exposure; it has triggered it. What pours through the crack it opens is the truth she no longer has the capacity to hold: the compartments she has maintained at such cost (Elena the mother, Elena the partner, Elena the lover, Elena the haunted wife) collapse into one another because the child who was part of what the secrecy protected is gone, and she has nothing left with which to perform.

Davide does not shout, and he does not withdraw his love. This is the change that completes the opera's moral architecture. He has lost the child he raised as his own, and now, in the same hour, he learns he has lost the certainty of Elena's love as well. But he does not weigh her betrayal and deliver a verdict. He tries to stay. He does not blame her. He wants to hold the marriage together and care for her through the wreckage.

The tragedy is that his grief is too large to stay contained. A man who has been the still centre of the household for years breaks, once, into ungoverned heat: grief at the dead child, grief at the sensed loss of her, fury at his own helplessness, none of it aimed at Elena and none of it cruel. But Elena's instrument for reading a man was calibrated across the years of her marriage. Vittorio taught her that intensity from a man is the thing that comes before harm. She does not hear Davide's grief as grief. She hears the pattern she was trained to hear. The contained man losing his grip is precisely what Vittorio looked like in the moments before violence, and her body responds before her judgement can. She asks him to leave. She needs space, distance, safety, and he has become, in her hearing only, the shape of danger.

Davide does not understand. He cannot see the timeline that runs from a wedding in 1950 to this room, the marriage that foreclosed her freedom and rewired her capacity to recognise safety. He does not know that her love calibrated him, in this moment, against a man he never met. He half-grasps it on the threshold, too late: a flicker of comprehension that the woman sending him away is not judging him and is not unloving but is in the grip of something planted in her long before he knew her. And still he goes, because she has asked him to, and because to refuse, to stay against her will, to override her, would be to become the very thing she fears. His leaving is an act of love. It is also the final proof that the marriage destroys everything it reaches, fifteen years on, through a door Vittorio never walked through.

Davide's aria is therefore not love withdrawn but love that stays and is refused. It is sung by a man who has done nothing wrong, who wants only to remain, and who is being asked to go by someone he still loves and cannot reach. He must never, for one bar, actually be frightening to the audience. They see his gentleness. They see her terror. They are not permitted to take a side. They grieve the gap.

Elena, alone, goes to Marco. She does not ask him to take her in; she goes to him as the only thing left. Marco, faced with the reality of what his return has caused, withdraws. He knew about Tommaso. He chose this anyway. His withdrawal is not the recoil of a man surprised by consequences but the harder thing: the recognition that he was never the answer, only the question, and that staying would compound rather than redeem.

Elena alone on stage. The final aria. This is a *scena ed aria* in the grand Italian tradition: opening *scena* (free, parlante at the edge of speech), moving into a cantabile section, a second lyrical movement after the first Vittorio intrusion, and a final lyrical close before the curtain.

This is not a death scene. This is not an apology. This is a woman arriving, ten years too late, at the clear sight of one moment in her life: the day she said yes to Vittorio. Her vocal line is the most sustained and lyrical in the opera. Not fractured. Not guilty. She is not pleading. She is looking backward at a single point.

**Scena** (free, in the empty apartment). Elena moves through the space. She names what is not there. Davide's chair. Tommaso's blanket. The orchestra is sparse.

**Cantabile (the backward look)**. Her vocal line settles into lyrical breath. She sees herself at eighteen in the village square, the evening Marco left for Bologna. She sees herself at twenty in her parents' parlour, her mother speaking of security, of the world as it is. She sees Vittorio at the wedding: handsome, well-regarded, apparently kind, a good match by every measure available to a young woman and her family in 1950. He was the man of her dreams. Not her fantasy, but by every evidence she had, her dream. She says this without bitterness. It is simply what was true.

**First Vittorio intrusion** (placed at the end of the cantabile):

> *Elena, tutto è finito.*
> *Il bambino, l'altro, il fuoco.*
> *Solo io resto nel silenzio,*
> *tuo principio, tua rovina.*

(Elena, everything is finished. / The child, the other, the fire. / Only I remain in the silence, / your beginning, your ruin.)

The words *tuo principio, tua rovina* (your beginning, your ruin) land as the aria's central truth. He was both. The day of the wedding was the beginning and the ruin simultaneously, and she could not have seen it.

**Second lyrical movement (the unbroken line)**. She sees now what the marriage set in motion. Tommaso's disability. Her leaving. The years in Trieste. Davide's love. Marco's return. The affair she entered because she was already Vittorio's before she could be anyone else's. Her child's death. The letter. Davide leaving. Marco withdrawing. All of it, from the moment she said yes, was one continuous line. There was never a second point at which she could have stepped out. She understands this not as excuse but as fact. What she could not have done was know at twenty what she knows now. The evidence was not there to be read. The man who seemed the man of her dreams was indistinguishable, at that moment, from the man she later discovered. And this, she sees, is the condition under which every such choice is made. Not by her alone. By everyone.

**Second Vittorio intrusion**, placed immediately before the final cadence:

> *Elena, sei mia per sempre.*

A single line, the opening words of Appearance 1, returning unchanged. The cruellest possible use of the device: the first words he sang to her in vision were always literally true. From the day of the wedding she was his forever. The melody that sounded seductive the first time, and grew darker as the meanings descended, returns here in its opening form and the audience understands that every version of these words, from husband to parasite to ruin, was always present in the first iteration. She could not have heard it. Nobody could.

**Final lyrical close**. Elena does not respond to the voice. The orchestra carries her, alone, into the final cadence. Her last phrases are lyrical and unflinching. She understands. She could not have done otherwise. Neither could anyone who had been her, at her age, with her knowledge. What she leaves the audience with is not regret (she had no available action that would have saved her) but clarity about the nature of the decision she once made. The audience, watching, is forced to confront the same truth about decisions they have made or will make: the single choice that determines the shape of a life is made on evidence that is always insufficient, and the cost of its insufficiency can be total.

The opera ends. She does not die on stage. She does not declare any intention. She simply is. This is the opera's instruction without preaching: no one on stage tells the audience what to conclude, and the instruction is not about avoiding Elena's later actions (which were not really hers to avoid) but about the weight and unknowability of the one choice she made at twenty. Every audience member who has chosen a life partner, or will, is being asked to feel how little they knew or know.

Curtain on a single figure. The stage picture is the echo of Rigoletto with the sack: a body in the centre, everything it loved gone, and the full weight of the one choice that determined everything now understood.

## Key Musical Moments

These are the twelve numbers that should anchor the score:

1. Act I, Scene 1: Opening village chorus; Elena and Marco's farewell duet; Elena's "longing" aria
2. Act I, Scene 2: Elena's aria of cautious hope; the wedding chorus (this melodic material returns transformed in Act III Scene 1)
3. Act I, Scene 3: Elena's lullaby-aria to Tommaso; the violent ensemble closing the act
4. Act II, Scene 1: Elena's "departure" aria at the station
5. Act II, Scene 2: Vittorio's nightmare-visitation; Davide's aria of love; Elena and Davide's duet
6. Act II, Scene 3: Elena's confession-duet with Marco about her present life; their reunion duet
7. Act III, Scene 1: Marco's aria of unease; Vittorio's vision-phrases; the five-movement Stabat Mater chorus (verses 1, 2, 8, 9, 14) layered against the transformed wedding melody
8. Act III, Scene 2: The shared ensemble over the dying child (Elena and Davide in undivided grief)
9. Act III, Scene 3: The funeral chorus (Requiem fragments)
10. Act III, Scene 3: The arrival of Marco's letter, Davide's wordless reading, and Elena's collapse into full confession (orchestral underscoring beneath near-silence, leading directly into the next number)
11. Act III, Scene 3: Davide's aria of love that stays and is refused
12. Act III, Scene 3: Elena's final aria (*scena ed aria*, interrupted twice by offstage Vittorio on the fixed melody before the final cadence)

## Thematic and Motivic Threads

Worth establishing early so they can transform across the opera:

- **The wedding melody**: the opera's central tune, first heard in Act I Scene 2 as a major-key celebration. Must be at the level of *Nessun Dorma* or *La donna è mobile*: culturally portable, immediately memorable. Returns in Act III Scene 1 slowed, harmonically darkened, in the lower strings beneath the Stabat Mater. The five Latin verses sit above the melody as a second layer of lament. By movement 2 (*pertransivit gladius*) the melody should be fragmenting; by movement 4 (verse 9) it returns more fully stated in a minor transformation; by movement 5 (verse 14) it dissolves into the unresolved closing chord. Compose this melody before anything else.
- **Marco's motif**: heard in Act I as something tender and incomplete; returns in Act III warped and dominant.
- **Tommaso's tone**: a single sustained pitch, sung or played, returning whenever he is present and audible to the audience even when he is silent on stage. The choice of pitch is critical (mid-range, lyrical, neither bright nor dark) because it has to bear repetition without becoming sentimental. The death moment in Act III Scene 2 is staged as the absence of this tone where it is expected. The audience will feel the silence before they consciously identify what is missing.
- **Vittorio's vision-music**: the critical device. Vittorio sings the same melodic phrase on every appearance (Act II Scene 2 nightmare, Act III Scene 1 vision, Act III Scene 3 offstage voice). The music itself does not darken; only the text does. The melody should be baritone-seductive, almost lyrical, a phrase that could be mistaken on first hearing for a husband's tender address. Its stability is the point. To an external observer nothing changes; his tone is constant. What descends is only the meaning of what he says. This mirrors the structure of a controlling relationship, where the surface manner stays loving while the inner reality degenerates. Across the three appearances the phrase is identical in pitches and rhythm; the only musical variation is the accompaniment, which may thin, darken, or disappear entirely by the final offstage intrusion, leaving the vocal line unaccompanied and therefore more exposed. This is the opposite of conventional antagonist writing and will be one of the most striking features of the score.
- **Davide's music**: warm, slow-moving, harmonically settled. The sound of an actual home. This is the deliberate opposite of Vittorio's seductive line, and it must remain so even in Act III Scene 3. When Elena mishears his grief as Vittorio-like, the resemblance must exist only in her hearing, never in the score's fact. The orchestra should stay with Davide, warm and unthreatening; his music must not borrow Vittorio's idiom, because to let it do so would tell the audience he really is Vittorio-like and break the scene. The distortion belongs to Elena's perception alone. Convey it through her vocal line, through a subjective shift of orchestral colour keyed to her, or through staging and light, so that the audience hears the truth (a grieving good man) while understanding that she hears the threat. This subjective split is one of the most demanding pieces of musical dramaturgy in the work.
- **Elena's vocal line**: most lyrical when alone with the child, with Davide, or in memory; most fractured in Act III when she is with Marco or lying to Davide.

The orchestra in 1950s setting allows for a range of stylistic options. A reference frame that fits the period and the dramatic register: Britten (especially *Peter Grimes* and *The Turn of the Screw*), Barber (*Vanessa*), Poulenc (*Dialogues des Carmélites*), late Janáček. Tonal foundation, modernist edges, conservative orchestra (no electronics, no extended techniques unless pointed). Strings dominant, woodwinds for the intimate scenes, brass for Vittorio and the violent moments. For Tommaso's sustained tone, consider a solo instrument that can hold without vibrato and without drawing excessive attention: cor anglais, muted horn, or a high cello harmonic.

## Decisions Made

The structural choices now locked in:

1. **Vittorio returns in vision** in Act II Scene 2 (nightmare, embodied), Act III Scene 1 (embodied), and as an offstage voice during Elena's final aria in Act III Scene 3 (two intrusions, both before the final cadence so the opera ends in her solitude).
2. **Vittorio's two on-stage visions are acted by the baritone himself**, not projected, not recorded, not doubled. The role is therefore substantial across three acts, not a cameo return. Casting will need a baritone with the stamina and the psychological range for this.
3. **Vittorio sings the same melodic phrase on every appearance.** The music does not darken. Only the text descends. The mechanism is the opposite of conventional antagonist writing: the audience hears the same seductive baritone line each time, and only the words tell them what is happening. This is one of the work's most original features and mirrors the structure of a controlling relationship, where surface manner stays loving while inner reality degenerates. Draft text across the three appearances is in the synopsis above and will be refined alongside the melody.
4. **Marco knows about Tommaso and Davide** before he and Elena become lovers. His moral position is not naive.
5. **The consummation is offstage**, with the chorus carrying the moment.
6. **The chorus laments rather than condemns.** Elena's tragedy is that one decision, the marriage at twenty to a man who seemed the love of her life, determined everything. Every action after that was the unfolding of what was already set. She could not have done otherwise after the wedding, and she could not have known at the wedding what she was choosing. She is not a pure victim (she does consummate the affair, she does lie to Davide) but the opera makes clear that those actions were the working-out of a fate she had already entered. The chorus mourns her. The text is the **Stabat Mater**, verses 1, 2, 8, 9, and 14, set in five movements against the transformed wedding melody.
7. **The chorus serves three functions**: villagers (Act I), unseen lamenting witnesses to the consummation (Act III Scene 1), funeral congregation (Act III Scene 3).
8. **The wedding chorus must be the central tune of the opera**, at the level of *Nessun Dorma* or *La donna è mobile*. **Compose the wedding tune first**, before any libretto work begins.
9. **Tommaso is present but profoundly limited**: physically on stage in Acts II and III. His "voice" is a single sustained tone, mid-range, neither bright nor dark. Its absence in Act III Scene 2 is his death.
10. **The libretto is in the high Italian operatic tradition**: verse, elevated diction, formal structures. Reference the librettists of the Verdi era (Piave, Cammarano, Boito) for register; reference Montale and Quasimodo for the period sensibility.
11. **Elena's final aria is a *scena ed aria* centred on a single backward look**: the day she said yes to Vittorio. She understands that moment clearly for the first time. She sees that Vittorio was, on all available evidence, the man of her dreams; that the evidence was inadequate; that nothing about the marriage's ruin was foreseeable from the point of decision; and that from the wedding onward every subsequent event was the unfolding of what was already set. This is her single recognition, not a catalogue of many. Her vocal line is sustained and lyrical, clear-eyed rather than agonised. The audience must leave the theatre sympathising with her entirely.
12. **The opera's instruction to the audience is about the weight and unknowability of the one choice she made at twenty.** Not about avoiding her later actions (which were not really hers to avoid) but about the condition under which she said yes to a man who then destroyed her. Every audience member who has chosen a life partner or will is being asked to feel how little they knew or know. The work does not preach this; it stages Elena's clarity and trusts the audience to transfer it to their own lives.
13. **Tommaso dies with Elena present.** Elena and Davide are out together when news reaches them; they return in time. Both parents are with the child as he dies. Elena's grief in this scene is whole and uncorrupted, the one untainted experience she is granted in the opera. The affair and the death are deliberately not bound together in time or place. The tragedy of the final scene is pure loss becoming the agent of ruin, not punishment arriving on cue.
14. **Marco's letter is the trigger of the exposure in Act III Scene 3.** Marco writes to Elena pouring out his feelings, not knowing the child has died; the letter arrives after the funeral. It is his structural counterpart to Elena's wedding: a single non-malicious act with catastrophic timing. Davide reaches the letter first and reads it wordlessly (or reads a single line and stops). The letter is never performed by an offstage voice. It triggers the exposure but does not cause it: what follows is Elena's collapse, the compartments merging because grief has stripped her of the strength to hold them. She confesses everything not because she is caught but because she can no longer perform. Marco remains catalyst, never author of the ruin.
15. **Davide does not withdraw his love; he is sent away.** He tries to stay and care for Elena through the wreckage, without blaming her. But his grief, at Tommaso's death and at the sensed loss of her, breaks once into ungoverned heat, and Elena, whose perception of men was calibrated by Vittorio, misreads that intensity as the prelude to harm. She asks him to leave because she needs space and safety, and he has become, in her hearing alone, the shape of danger. He does not understand the timeline running from her marriage to this moment, the lack of true choice that rewired her, until he is on the threshold and it is too late. He goes anyway, because to stay against her will would be to become the thing she fears. His aria is love that stays and is refused, not love withdrawn. This removes the last trace of moral verdict from the close: nobody judges Elena; the catastrophe is two people in grief who cannot reach each other, and the marriage reaching forward fifteen years to destroy a love that was never a threat. The scene must be staged so that the audience sees Davide's gentleness and Elena's terror with equal clarity and is not permitted to take a side.

## Next Decisions for the Libretto Stage

These remain to be resolved:

1. **The Vittorio melody itself**: a seductive baritone phrase, four ottonari long, that could be mistaken on first hearing for a husband's tender address. Must scan against all three drafted texts (Act II Scene 2, Act III Scene 1, Act III Scene 3 first intrusion) and against the single-line return in Act III Scene 3 second intrusion. Compose alongside or immediately after the wedding tune.
2. **Refinement of Vittorio's Italian text**: the drafts above are structurally correct (ottonari, matching stresses) but the specific words need polishing for sound, for prosody under the melody once composed, and for the exact calibration of the descent from appearance to appearance.
3. **The Stabat Mater setting**: which musical models to draw on (Pergolesi, Rossini, Poulenc), what textures, what harmonic language for the overlay against the wedding melody. To be worked out during composition.
4. **The pitch of Tommaso's tone**: specific note and specific instrument or voice.
5. **The staging of the Davide departure (Act III Scene 3)**: this is the work's hardest scene and a flagged risk. It rests on a knife-edge. If Davide reads as genuinely menacing, the audience thinks Elena is right to send him away and the tragedy collapses into a correct decision. If she reads as paranoid or cruel, the blame the opera has spent three acts dispelling rushes back at the final moment. The audience must see his gentleness and her terror with equal clarity and grieve the gap rather than take a side. This depends almost entirely on the singer playing Davide: his grief-intensity must be visibly grief and never actually frightening to anyone but Elena. It also depends on the subjective-mishearing device in the orchestration (see Davide's musical thread). Resolve the staging and the musical means together; they are one problem.
6. **The full libretto**: to be written scene by scene once the two core melodies (wedding and Vittorio) are settled. The libretto cannot sensibly be written before those exist because the prosody of the sung lines depends on the melodic contours.

## Notes on Avoiding Predecessors and Finding Tradition

Two operas occupy almost exactly this territory and should be studied, not as templates but as cautionary maps:

- **Janáček, *Káťa Kabanová***: trapped wife, brutal marriage (here from the mother-in-law), affair, destruction. Yours differs in the disabled child, the genuine happiness with Davide, and the catalyst rather than seducer function of the returning figure. Káťa drowns herself. Elena does not.
- **Janáček, *Jenůfa***: hinges on the death of a child, the moral weight of a woman's choices, and the possibility of love after ruin. Yours shares the child's death but inverts the ending: Jenůfa finds love, Elena loses it and is left in recognition.

Your work's distinctness rests on three things Janáček does not have: the catalyst (not seducer) function of the original love, the disabled rather than infant child, and the reflective rather than redemptive close.

The tradition this opera sits in is older and larger than Janáček. It is the tradition of tragedy as moral instruction without moral instruction being spoken: Greek tragedy (Medea, Phaedra, Electra), the great nineteenth-century novel (*Anna Karenina*, *Madame Bovary*, *Effi Briest*), Strindberg and Ibsen (*Miss Julie*, *Hedda Gabler*). What your work specifically shares with these is the recognition that the catastrophe unfolds from a single early act (a marriage, a refusal, a concealment) that determines everything afterwards, and that the heroine's later actions are the working-out rather than the authoring of her fate. The operatic equivalents are rarer because opera tends toward the redemptive (the dying soprano transfigured by love, by faith, by sacrifice). The absence of transfiguration at your close is therefore one of the work's most distinctive features. The audience does not get to be consoled. They get the cold clarity of recognising that the most consequential decision in a life is made on evidence that is always insufficient, and they are being asked to hold that recognition against decisions of their own.
