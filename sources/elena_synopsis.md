# Elena: Synopsis

### *Tragedia lirica in tre atti*

*Elena* is the story of a single decision and the life that unfolds from it. The decision is made by a young woman of twenty, on the best evidence available to her, with the approval of everyone who loves her. It proves to be the wrong decision. But it could not have been known to be wrong, and the opera asks its audience to hold that distinction throughout: that a choice can be both catastrophic and entirely without blame.

## Act I

In a village in the Veneto in 1948, Elena is eighteen. She and Marco, friends since childhood, are easy in one another's company, and there is something in that friendship neither of them names before Marco leaves for Bologna to study medicine. They part with a vague promise to write. Elena waits. No letters come.

Two years later a marriage is proposed for her: Vittorio, son of a Verona industrialist. By every measure available to Elena and her family, Vittorio is an excellent man. He is prosperous, well-regarded, courteous, from a good family, and he offers security and a future. Elena's mother, who loves her, urges the match. Elena accepts it, not as a grey consolation but with genuine hope, because the man before her appears to be everything a young woman of her time and place could wish for. There is no warning to be read. On all the evidence, the man at the wedding is the man of her dreams.

The marriage darkens. Vittorio reveals himself as controlling, then cruel, then violent. None of this was visible beforehand; none of it could have been foreseen. A child, Tommaso, is born with a severe disability, and Vittorio resents the boy openly. By 1953 Elena understands that she must leave, and she does, taking Tommaso with her.

## Act II

Elena escapes to Trieste and builds a modest life: work as a seamstress, a small flat, the daily care of her son. In the same building lives Davide, a schoolteacher, an unassuming and genuinely good man. He befriends Elena, plays with Tommaso, asks for nothing. Over two years a real love grows between them, and they come to live as husband and wife in everything but law, since Elena remains married to Vittorio. For the first time, Elena is happy. Tommaso is fragile but adored by them both.

Then Marco returns. He has taken a hospital post in Trieste. Their reunion reactivates a longing Elena had buried years before. She tells him the truth about her life, her child, and Davide; he does not withdraw. They agree to meet again.

## Act III

Over some months Elena and Marco are drawn closer, and an affair follows. It is less a choice made freely than the working-out of everything the marriage set in motion. Elena turns to Marco because she had been Vittorio's since she was twenty, and the version of herself that might once have loved freely had been foreclosed long before.

One evening, while Elena and Davide are out together, Tommaso collapses. They return in time, and both of them are with the child as he dies. His death is a crisis of the condition he was born with, unconnected to anything anyone did or failed to do. Elena's grief is whole and uncorrupted. In this one moment she is simply a mother losing her son.

After the funeral a letter arrives from Marco. He wrote it without knowing the child had died, an outpouring of his feelings posted into the worst imaginable moment. Davide reads it first. Elena, broken by grief and no longer able to hold the separate compartments of her life apart, does not deny anything. She tells Davide everything.

Davide does not rage, and he does not withdraw his love. He tries to stay. He does not blame her; he wants to hold the marriage together and care for her through the wreckage. But his grief, at the dead child and at the sensed loss of her, breaks once into ungoverned heat. It is grief, not cruelty, and none of it is aimed at her. Elena, however, learned across the years of her marriage that intensity from a man is the thing that comes before harm. She does not hear Davide's grief as grief. She hears the pattern Vittorio taught her, and her body answers before her judgement can. She asks him to leave. He does not understand the timeline that runs from a wedding in 1950 to this room, and he grasps it only on the threshold, too late. He goes anyway, because to stay against her will would be to become the very thing she fears. His leaving is an act of love. Elena, now alone, turns to Marco, who, seeing what his return has caused, also withdraws.

Elena is left alone. In a final aria she looks back at the one moment that determined all of it: the day she said yes to Vittorio. She understands, clearly and without self-pity, that she chose well by every measure she had, that the evidence of what Vittorio would become simply was not there to be read, and that nothing she did afterward was ever truly free of that first, reasonable, irreversible decision.

## On Elena's Innocence

The opera attaches no blame to Elena. She is not a woman who chose badly through weakness, vanity, or want of care. She is a woman who made the most consequential decision of her life at twenty, on good evidence, with the support of everyone who loved her, and who could not have known what that decision contained. Her later actions, the affair, the deception of Davide, are the unfolding of what the marriage set in motion rather than free departures from it. The marriage cost her more than her freedom. It rewired her, so that years later she could no longer tell tenderness from threat, and lost the one love that was never a danger because she had been trained to flinch from intensity itself. Nobody in the opera judges her. Davide does not leave because he weighs her and finds her wanting; he leaves because she asks him to, and she asks because the marriage reached forward fifteen years and put fear where trust should have been. The tragedy is not a moral failing. It is the condition under which every person chooses a husband, a wife, a life: the evidence is always insufficient, and the cost of its insufficiency can be total. The audience is not asked to judge her. They are asked to recognise her, and to feel, leaving the theatre, how much of their own lives has rested on choices made in the same darkness.
